Bertrand Russell (1872–1970): From Trinity Fellowship to Logicism¶

Mathematical & Philosophical Formation at Cambridge (1890–1903)

Bertrand Arthur William Russell (18 May 1872 – 2 February 1970), 3rd Earl Russell, entered Trinity College, Cambridge in October 1890 as a scholar in the Mathematical Tripos (7th Wrangler, 1893) before turning to the Moral Sciences Tripos (First Class with distinction, 1894). Throughout the crucial decade 1894–1903, the foundations of geometry formed the central crucible in which Russell forged his philosophy of mathematics.

Note

Authorial Distinction in GEOMETOR: This repository (geometor/bertrand-russell) is dedicated exclusively to the British mathematician, logician, and philosopher Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)—the author cited throughout geometor/hoenen and geometor/euclid—and is wholly distinct from the American artist and cosmologist Walter Russell (1871–1963).

The 1895 Trinity Dissertation and the 1897 Essay¶

Under the influence of his Cambridge tutor James Ward, his Neo-Hegelian colleague John McTaggart Ellis McTaggart (to whom the 1897 volume is dedicated), Francis Herbert Bradley, and his mentor and future collaborator Alfred North Whitehead, Russell submitted his Trinity Fellowship dissertation on the foundations of geometry in August 1895. Expanded from lectures delivered at Bryn Mawr College and Johns Hopkins University in late 1896, it appeared from Cambridge University Press in May 1897 as An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry (Codex II).

In the 1897 Essay, Russell sought to modernize Immanuel Kant’s Transcendental Aesthetic (Critique of Pure Reason) in light of nineteenth-century metageometry. Accepting the logical consistency of hyperbolic (Lobachevsky, Bolyai), spherical (Riemann), and elliptic (Klein) geometries, Russell argued that pure projective geometry and the general axioms of constant-curvature metrical space (free mobility, finite integral dimensions, and two-point distance) are a priori conditions of any possible form of externality, whereas the specific Euclidean value zero of the space-constant and three-dimensionality are purely empirical facts.

The Revolt into Pluralism and the Paris Congress of 1900¶

Between 1898 and 1900, two decisive intellectual encounters dismantled the neo-Kantian and Neo-Hegelian framework of the 1897 Essay:

  1. Analytic Realism and the Logic of Relations (1898–1899): Together with G. E. Moore, Russell rejected Idealist internal relations and transcendental psychology, adopting a pluralistic realism in which asymmetric relations (such as spatial order, betweenness, and greater/less) are irreducible, objective realities rather than appearances grounded in monistic substances. Simultaneously, Henri Poincaré’s penetrating 1899 critique in the Revue de Métaphysique et de Morale exposed operational gaps in Russell’s 1897 definitions of distance and free mobility (Russell 1899).

  2. Peano, Pieri, and Cantor at Paris (July–August 1900): At the International Congress of Philosophy in Paris in August 1900, Russell witnessed Giuseppe Peano and his Italian school (Mario Pieri, Cesare Burali-Forti, Alessandro Padoa) wield symbolic relational logic with unprecedented precision. Mastering Peano’s symbolic notation, Pieri’s projective and metrical memoirs, and Georg Cantor’s transfinite continuum within months, Russell drafted the bulk of The Principles of Mathematics in the autumn of 1900, completing the manuscript for Cambridge University Press in December 1902 (published May 1903; Codex I).

Legacy in Mathematical Philosophy¶

Part VI (Space, Chapters XLIV–LII, §§ 351–436) of the 1903 Principles marks one of the clearest watersheds in modern foundations of geometry:

  • Every specific geometry (projective, descriptive, Euclidean, hyperbolic, elliptic, Clifford-Klein) becomes the definition of a class of relational structures, while pure geometry asserts strictly hypothetical formal implications from those definitions.

  • The dialectical “antinomy of the point” that closed the 1897 Essay (EFG §§ 189–204) is dissolved through Cantorian set theory and Russell’s critique of Lotze’s absolute-space arguments (PoM Ch. LI, §§ 423–431).

  • Russell’s sharp logical dissection of Euclid’s tacit continuity and congruence postulates in Chapter XLVII (§§ 389–391) immediately entered the canonical literature on Euclid through Sir Thomas L. Heath’s 1908 commentary and later provoked Fr. Petrus Hoenen, S.J.’s monumental Thomistic response in De Noetica Geometriae (1954).
