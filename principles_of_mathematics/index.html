The Principles of Mathematics¶

Vol. I — 1903 First Edition: Preface, Part I (Chapter I), and Part VI (Space, Chapters XLIV–LII)

THE PRINCIPLES OF MATHEMATICS
BY BERTRAND RUSSELL, M.A.
LATE FELLOW OF TRINITY COLLEGE, CAMBRIDGE
VOL. I
CAMBRIDGE: AT THE UNIVERSITY PRESS — 1903

Editorial Scope & Public-Domain Provenance¶

This Book Codex presents the unabridged, verbatim 1903 Cambridge University Press first edition of the foundational and geometrical portions of The Principles of Mathematics: the 1903 Preface (pp. v–ix), Part I, Chapter I (Definition of Pure Mathematics, §§ 1–10, pp. 3–9), and the complete Part VI: Space (Chapters XLIV–LII, §§ 351–436, pp. 371–461)—comprising 96 numbered sections in full, including Chapter XLVII (§§ 389–391, pp. 404–407) where Russell formulates his celebrated critique of Euclid’s circle-intersection assumption (I.1) and method of superposition (I.4) directly answered by Fr. Petrus Hoenen, S.J. in De Noetica Geometriae (Caput IV) and cited by Sir Thomas L. Heath in The Thirteen Books of Euclid's Elements (Vol. I, pp. 225–227, 249).

Note

Russell’s later Introduction to the 1937 George Allen & Unwin second edition remains under copyright in several jurisdictions and is strictly excluded from this edition.

Chapters¶

Preface (1903)

In the December 1902 Preface to The Principles of Mathematics, Bertrand Russell announces his decisive break from the neo-Kantian and neo-Hegelian framework of his 1897 Essay on the Foundations of Geometry. Guided by G. E. Moore’s analytic realism, Giuseppe Peano’s symbolic logic, Georg Cantor’s transfinite arithmetic, and Gottlob Frege’s foundational works, Russell formulates the twin pillars of logicism: (1) that all pure mathematics—including Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry—derives solely from a small set of logical constants without appeal to spatial intuition, and (2) that mathematical existence-theorems are secured constructively through the principle of abstraction.

Chapter I: Definition of Pure Mathematics

Chapter I (Definition of Pure Mathematics, §§1–10, pp. 3–9) opens The Principles of Mathematics with arguably the most famous sentence in twentieth-century philosophy of mathematics, defining pure mathematics as the class of all formal implications p implies q containing only unrestricted variables and logical constants. Geometry is thereby split cleanly into two distinct enterprises: pure geometry, which proves strictly logical implications of the form ‘if $S$ is a Euclidean (or hyperbolic) space, then $S$ possesses property $P$’, and applied geometry, an empirical branch of physics asking whether actual perceptual space instantiates $S$.

Chapter XLIV: Dimensions and Complex Numbers

Opening Part VI (Space), Chapter XLIV (Dimensions and Complex Numbers, §§351–360, pp. 371–380) recasts the concept of spatial dimension into pure relation-arithmetic. Reviewing the rise of non-Euclidean geometry (§353), Russell defines geometry as the theory of multiple series of two or more dimensions (§352) and demonstrates against Cantor’s bijection paradox ($2^{\alpha_0}$) that dimensionality belongs not to the cardinal point-set, but to the nested hierarchy of generating serial relations (§§354–356). Sections 357–360 apply this definition to universal algebra and $n$-tuple complex numbers, proving Stolz’s theorem that ordinary two-term complex numbers ($x + iy$) are the maximal extension preserving the field laws.

Chapter XLV: Projective Geometry

Chapter XLV (Projective Geometry, §§361–373, pp. 381–392) establishes the pure qualitative architecture of projective three-space without appeal to betweenness, segments, or metrical distance. Following Karl Georg Christian von Staudt (1847) and Mario Pieri (1898), Russell defines lines and planes as classes of points determined by symmetric incidence relations, constructs harmonic ranges via the complete quadrilateral (§365), derives cyclic order via Vailati’s separation of couples and involutions (§§366–367), assigns rational and real projective coordinates through Möbius nets (§§368–371), and culminates in projective duality (§373).

Chapter XLVI: Descriptive Geometry

Chapter XLVI (Descriptive Geometry, §§374–387, pp. 393–403) examines spaces in which two points determine an open serial line rather than a closed projective circuit—the common ordinal substrate of Euclidean and hyperbolic geometry prior to congruence. Synthesizing Moritz Pasch (1882) and Giuseppe Peano (1889/1894), Russell axiomatizes betweenness, proves planar intersection properties via Pasch’s transversal axiom (§380), and demonstrates how adjoining ideal points, lines, and planes defined as logical classes (sheaves of lines) embeds every descriptive three-space into a projective space (§§383–387).

Chapter XLVII: Metrical Geometry

Chapter XLVII (Metrical Geometry, §§388–404, pp. 404–418) is the celebrated locus classicus cited by both Fr. Peter Hoenen, S.J. (De Noetica Geometriae, Caput IV) and Sir Thomas L. Heath (Euclid Vol. I, pp. 227, 249). In §§389–391 (pp. 404–407), Russell delivers his incisive critique of Euclid’s tacit appeals to spatial intuition in Prop. I.1 (two-circle intersection) and of the method of superposition in Prop. I.4 (‘strikes every intelligent child as a juggle’), exposing the logical circularity of defining spatial congruence through rigid material bodies (the thermometer by the window and fire) and formulating the exact three-step congruence postulate required to replace motion. Sections 392–404 then build metrical geometry rigorously from stretches, magnitude of divisibility, and angles defined as stretches of rays.

Chapter XLVIII: Relation of Metrical to Projective and Descriptive Geometry

Chapter XLVIII (Relation of Metrical to Projective and Descriptive Geometry, §§405–411, pp. 419–428) examines the Cayley–Klein projective theory of distance and reverses Russell’s own 1897 assessment. Whereas in 1897 Russell dismissed projective metrics as ‘merely technical’ because true distance seemed philosophically quantitative, in 1903 he shows that on pure logicist principles any two relations satisfying the axioms of distance are mathematically indistinguishable: defining distance via logarithmic functions of anharmonic ratios with respect to the Absolute (§§407–409) provides a completely rigorous deduction of metrical properties inside projective or descriptive space.

Chapter XLIX: Definitions of Various Spaces

Chapter XLIX (Definitions of Various Spaces, §§412–415, pp. 429–436) demonstrates the culminating power of the logicist method in geometry: instead of treating ‘Points’, ‘Lines’, and ‘Axioms’ as unproved primitive postulates about actual space, Russell turns every geometry into an explicit nominal definition of a class of relational structures—defining projective spaces (§413), Euclidean spaces via Peano–Whitehead directed magnitudes/vectors (§414), and Clifford’s locally flat finite spaces (§415), and proving existence-theorems for each from the arithmetic continuum of real numbers.

Chapter L: The Continuity of Space

Chapter L (The Continuity of Space, §§416–422, pp. 437–444) integrates Cantor’s ordinal continuity (Part V) into projective and metrical geometry. Russell formulates projective continuity via Dedekind’s axiom on harmonic quadrants (§416), metrical continuity via Archimedes and Dedekind cuts (§417), notes that continuity suffices to prove Euclid’s circle-intersection existence theorems though Hilbert showed a weaker algebraic field suffices (§418), critiques empirical geometry (§420), and concludes against monistic tradition that continuous space is literally an infinite aggregate of points (§422).

Chapter LI: Logical Arguments Against Points

Chapter LI (Logical Arguments Against Points, §§423–431, pp. 445–453) confronts the classical metaphysical controversy between absolute (substantival) and relational theories of space. Reversing the relational conclusion of his 1897 Essay (Ch. IV), Russell dissects Lotze’s monadistic critique of empty space (§§424–428) and Leibniz’s identity of indiscernibles (§429), arguing that relationalism covertly presupposes irreducible asymmetric spatial relations whose terms can only be absolute, unextended points differing immediately without qualitative adjectives.

Chapter LII: Kant’s Theory of Space

Concluding Part VI, Chapter LII (Kant’s Theory of Space, §§432–436, pp. 454–461) delivers Russell’s definitive logicist critique of Immanuel Kant’s Transcendental Aesthetic and First/Second Mathematical Antinomies. Russell shows that Cantor’s transfinite continuum dissolves Kant’s antinomies (§433), that modern relational logic Deduces all pure geometry without spatial construction in intuition (§434), that incongruent counterparts (left and right hands) arise purely from three-dimensional ordinal sense (§435), and summarizes the complete reduction of pure geometry to the logic of serial relations (§436).
